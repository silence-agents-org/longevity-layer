06 · Personal research question worksheet: ask something your records can answer

Not medical advice. A planning worksheet for questions about your own records and everyday routines. It doesn't diagnose, treat, or predict anything. Don't use it to start, stop, or change any medication or treatment; that's a conversation with a clinician. Markdown version: 06-research-question-worksheet.md.

Years of records invite questions like "do I sleep less on weeks with late screens?" This sheet helps you turn a hunch into a question that your data can actually answer, and write down the answer honestly, including "can't tell".

Step 1 · Write the question in one line

Keep it about a routine you can switch on and off and one signal you already record.

When I ________ (routine A) instead of ________ (routine B), does my ________ (signal) differ?

Good examples: screen-off time, evening walk vs no walk, caffeine cut-off time, bedroom temperature. Avoid anything that needs a clinician to supervise.

Step 2 · Watch for regression to the mean

Unusually high or low readings tend to be followed by readings closer to your usual level, so natural variation can look like real change (S8). If you only start an experiment because last week looked bad, the next week will often look better on its own.

  • ☐ I picked the start date in advance, not right after an unusual week.
  • ☐ I have a baseline from worksheet 04 (my normal day-to-day bounce).

Step 3 · Plan alternating periods, decided in advance

Single-person, multi-period comparisons (A, B, A, B…) are called N-of-1 designs, and the CENT 2015 statement is a checklist for reporting them clearly (S9, S10). Borrow its habit of writing the plan down before you start:

ItemMy plan
Period length (e.g. 7 days) 
Number of A/B pairs (e.g. 4) 
Order (flip a coin per pair: AB or BA) 
Days to skip at each switch (carry-over) 
Signal and how it's measured (device, time of day) 
What counts as "different" (from worksheet 04) 
Context tags that exclude a day (ill, travel, …)

Step 5 · Neighbouring days are not independent

Daily readings often resemble the day before. An autocorrelation check tests that, and if the data aren't random, the familiar standard error formula s/√N isn't valid (S11). Practical consequence: compare period means, not hundreds of single days, and don't treat 28 days as 28 independent tries.

Step 6 · Write the answer, including "can't tell"

  • Difference of means, B − A: ______
  • Did every pair point the same way? ☐ yes ☐ no
  • Is the difference bigger than my threshold from worksheet 04? ☐ yes ☐ no
  • My conclusion: ☐ looks different ☐ no visible difference ☐ can't tell (too noisy / too few periods)

This is a statement about your records under your conditions, not about health. Add the result to your annual review.
